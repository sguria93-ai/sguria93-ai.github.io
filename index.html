Evaluate the Trigonometric Limit

Compute the following limit without using numerical approximation tables:

$$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos(4x)}{x^2}$$
Method 1: Using L'Hôpital's Rule (Form 0/0)

Differentiating the numerator and denominator with respect to $x$:

$$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{4\sin(4x)}{2x} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{2\sin(4x)}{x}$$

Applying L'Hôpital a second time:

$$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{8\cos(4x)}{1} = 8\cos(0) = 8$$
Method 2: Using Taylor Series Expansion

Recall $\cos(\theta) = 1 - \frac{\theta^2}{2} + \mathcal{O}(\theta^4)$. Substitute $\theta = 4x$:

$$\cos(4x) = 1 - \frac{16x^2}{2} + \dots = 1 - 8x^2 + \dots$$ $$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - (1 - 8x^2)}{x^2} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{8x^2}{x^2} = 8$$
Final Answer: 8

Eigenvalues of a $2 \times 2$ Matrix

Find the eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$ (with $\lambda_1 < \lambda_2$) of the square matrix: $$A = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 4 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$$

Answer ($\lambda_1, \lambda_2$):

Step 1: Characteristic equation $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$:

$$\det \begin{pmatrix} 5-\lambda & 4 \\ 1 & 2-\lambda \end{pmatrix} = 0$$

Step 2: Expand determinant:

$$(5-\lambda)(2-\lambda) - (4)(1) = \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 - 4 = \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 6 = 0$$

Step 3: Factor:

$$(\lambda - 1)(\lambda - 6) = 0 \implies \lambda_1 = 1, \quad \lambda_2 = 6$$

Vertex and Maximum of a Quadratic Function

Find the maximum value of $f(x) = -3x^2 + 18x - 12$.

Maximum Value $f_{\max}$:

Method 1: Completing the square:

$$f(x) = -3(x^2 - 6x) - 12 = -3\left((x - 3)^2 - 9\right) - 12 = -3(x - 3)^2 + 27 - 12 = -3(x - 3)^2 + 15$$

The vertex is at $(3, 15)$, so the maximum value is $15$.

Integration by Parts with Trigonometric Function

Evaluate the definite integral: $$\int_{0}^{\pi} x \sin(x) \, dx$$

Value (Exact or π):

Step 1: By parts, let $u = x \implies du = dx$ and $dv = \sin(x)dx \implies v = -\cos(x)$.

$$\int_{0}^{\pi} x \sin(x)dx = \left[ -x \cos(x) \right]_{0}^{\pi} - \int_{0}^{\pi} (-\cos(x))dx$$

Step 2: Evaluate the boundary terms:

$$= \left( -\pi \cos(\pi) - (-0 \cos(0)) \right) + \int_{0}^{\pi} \cos(x)dx$$ $$= -\pi(-1) + \left[ \sin(x) \right]_{0}^{\pi} = \pi + (0 - 0) = \pi$$

Logarithmic Differentiation

Find the slope of the tangent line to the curve $y = x^x$ at $x = 1$.

Slope $y'(1)$:

Step 1: Take the natural log of both sides: $\ln(y) = \ln(x^x) = x \ln(x)$.

Step 2: Differentiate implicitly with respect to $x$:

$$\frac{1}{y} \frac{dy}{dx} = 1 \cdot \ln(x) + x \cdot \frac{1}{x} = \ln(x) + 1$$ $$\frac{dy}{dx} = y (\ln(x) + 1) = x^x (\ln(x) + 1)$$

Step 3: Evaluate at $x = 1$:

$$y'(1) = 1^1 (\ln(1) + 1) = 1(0 + 1) = 1$$

Bayesian Inference Difficulty: Medium

False Positive Paradox

A disease has a prevalence of $1\%$ ($P(D) = 0.01$). A laboratory test has a sensitivity of $95\%$ ($P(+|D) = 0.95$) and a false positive rate of $5\%$ ($P(+|D^c) = 0.05$). If a person tests positive, what is the probability they actually have the disease $P(D|+)$?

Posterior Probability (approx % or decimal):

Step 1: Total probability of testing positive $P(+)$:

$$P(+) = P(+|D)P(D) + P(+|D^c)P(D^c) = (0.95)(0.01) + (0.05)(0.99) = 0.0095 + 0.0495 = 0.059$$

Step 2: Apply Bayes' Rule:

$$P(D|+) = \frac{P(+|D)P(D)}{P(+)} = \frac{0.0095}{0.059} = \frac{95}{590} \approx 0.161 \quad (16.1\%)$$
